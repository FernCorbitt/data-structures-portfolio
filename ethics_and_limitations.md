# Ethics and Limitations

## Inconsistency Across the League
The biggest limitation by far is the inevitable and extreme inconsistency of baseball, especially when using data over multiple years. Though it is extremely rare for teams to make changes to stadium dimensions, a team almost never has the exact same roster they finished the previous season with. 

## Team Skill
A potential bias would be how good a team is, both in their home park and on the road. They will have the most games at their park over the full season, which may lead to outliers. Take the Colorado Rockies home stadium Coors Field for example: the stadium is roughly 5000 feet above sea level, leading to people expecting high offensive outputs. But in recent years, the Rockies have been one of the worst teams in baseball and have horrible offensive stats, suggesting Coors Field may be a pitcher friendly park, which couldn’t be further from the truth.
Additionally, no matter how good a team is, they will have long stretches of failure, at least in the box score. If that happens to line up with games at hitter-friendly parks, it may create a similar false narrative to Coors, but across all stadiums.

## Long Season
Due to the 162-gameseason, all stats, not just offensive ones, can change drastically over time. The offensive changes are more apparent and are often tied to a single team's performance, but gameplay strategies and changes across the league can also affect the dataset. Over the past few years, many players and teams are prioritizing power hitters, rather than signing a couple of players that may complement each other with speed, contact, defense, etc. 
But this power change has led to a large increase in strikeouts across the entire league. And depending on the team's skill, this could “shuffle” the top ~5 best and worst-hitting parks, and we may be working with inaccurate or outdated information.
